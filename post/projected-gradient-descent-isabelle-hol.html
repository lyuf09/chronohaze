从子模优化之后继续往前：Projected Gradient Descent in Isabelle/HOL

时间线说明

Originally posted: 2026年5月22日。本笔记早于子模贪心条目在 2026年5月26日正式发表于 AFP，因此“继续往前”指研究项目的延续，不是“发表之后”。

Submitted: 2026年7月2日 · Published in AFP: 2026年7月2日

我选 projected gradient descent（PGD）来做形式化，主要是因为算法本身不复杂，但证明收敛时会同时用到光滑性、凸性和投影几何。我想把这些部分分开整理，而不只是写出最后的收敛定理。

这个项目后来以 First-Order Methods for Smooth Convex Optimization in Isabelle/HOL 为题发表于 AFP。正式条目不仅包含 PGD，还包括普通 gradient descent 的收敛分析、projected-gradient mapping 的残差和最优性结果，以及强凸条件下的线性收敛定理。这篇主要记录我是怎么组织这些证明的。

为什么选 PGD

普通 gradient descent 的迭代是：

x_{n+1} = x_n - alpha * G x_n

如果要求迭代点留在可行集合 C 内，就再加一步投影：

x_{n+1} = P_C (x_n - alpha * G x_n)

其中 G 表示梯度，P_C 是到非空闭凸集 C 的 metric projection。写出迭代式很简单，真正麻烦的是后面每一步估计究竟需要哪些条件。

例如，要从投影步骤得到变分不等式，必须先处理好投影的定义与集合的性质。函数的 smoothness 也有不同的表述：证明下降时用 quadratic upper bound 很方便，但其他结果可能写成 Lipschitz gradient。我的做法是先把这些关系单独证明，再在具体算法中调用。

我怎么拆分这些证明

仓库中的 Isabelle theories 大致按照证明依赖来安排，而不是按最终定理逐个堆起来。

最基础的是 gradient、convex differentiability 和 smoothness，包括凸可微函数的一阶不等式、光滑函数的二次上界，以及相关假设之间的联系。然后是与具体算法无关的下降、有限和、平均值和 telescoping 引理。这样到了收敛证明时，不用为每个算法重写一遍相同的求和步骤。

接下来先做无约束的 gradient descent：由单步下降估计得到光滑凸情形下的 O(1/N) 函数值界，并处理相关的 gradient residual 结果。

PGD 则多了一层 projection geometry。需要先建立 metric projection 的性质和相应的 variational inequality，之后才能证明迭代的可行性、单步估计和有限步收敛界。

再往后是 projected-gradient mapping。这里不能只说函数值在下降，还需要把 mapping 的残差和一阶最优性条件联系起来，得到相应的 optimality certificate 与 epsilon-stationarity 复杂度界。最后，在额外的 strong convexity 假设下，再证明距离与函数值的线性收敛结果。

我比较喜欢 residual 这一部分，因为它回答的是一个更具体的问题：除了函数值接近最优值，我们能不能从算法的某一步直接得到一个可检查的一阶最优性指标？当然，指标具体能说明什么，还要看定理里用到了哪些假设。

为什么要单独整理 Main_Results.thy

我不想让后续使用这些结果的人，为了找一个 PGD 收敛定理就必须读完整个项目。所以这次把主要结论放进 Main_Results.thy，作为相对统一的导入入口。

这里集中提供 gradient descent 和 PGD 的收敛界、projected-gradient mapping 的最优性与残差结论，以及强凸情形的线性收敛定理。各个证明仍然放在对应的 theory 里，但使用时可以优先从 Main_Results 找到需要的结果。

这不只是改几个名称。如果以后我调整内部引理、拆分文件，尽量保持对外的 theorem aliases 稳定，就不必让依赖这些定理的其他项目跟着修改。

和之前子模贪心项目的联系

子模贪心项目里，我把具体的 greedy construction 与抽象的单步条件分开，让 lazy greedy 能复用 classical greedy 的近似证明。PGD 这里也有类似的考虑，只不过要拆开的变成 convexity、smoothness、projection、descent estimates 和 convergence rates。

我想弄清楚的是：哪些推导适用于一般下降序列，哪些必须用到投影，哪些结论又额外依赖强凸性。把条件写清楚，比把所有东西塞进一个大证明里更方便检查，也更方便以后复用。

发表前做了什么

这个 AFP 条目还包含 quadratic 和 projected quadratic 的例子。提交前的最后一段时间，我主要在统一定理名称、整理 Main_Results.thy、检查 theory 依赖和 build，并完成 AFP 需要的文档与参考文献，没有继续往里面加新算法。

这次做完以后，我在看一阶优化算法时，会更注意每条收敛结论具体用到了哪些条件，也会先想想哪些中间引理可以独立出来，而不是一上来就照着纸面证明逐行写进 Isabelle。

Moving Forward After Submodular Optimization: Projected Gradient Descent in Isabelle/HOL

Feier Lyu · Originally posted May 22, 2026 · 5 min read

Tags: Isabelle/HOL · Optimization · Formalisation · AFP

Publication update — August 2026

This project has now been accepted and published in the Archive of Formal Proofs as First-Order Methods for Smooth Convex Optimization in Isabelle/HOL. The published entry is available here.

View AFP Entry →

Chronology note

Originally posted: May 22, 2026. This note predates the submodular-greedy entry's AFP publication on May 26, so “moving forward” refers to continuing the research program, not to work begun after publication.

Submitted: July 2, 2026 · Published in AFP: July 2, 2026

I chose projected gradient descent (PGD) for this formalisation because the algorithm is simple, but its convergence proof brings together smoothness, convexity, and projection geometry. I wanted to formalise those parts separately, rather than focus only on the final convergence theorem.

The project was later published in the AFP as First-Order Methods for Smooth Convex Optimization in Isabelle/HOL. The entry covers more than PGD: it includes convergence results for ordinary gradient descent, residual and optimality results for the projected-gradient mapping, and linear rates under strong convexity. This note is about how I organised the proofs.

Why PGD

The usual gradient descent iteration is:

x_{n+1} = x_n - alpha * G x_n

With a constraint set C, we add a projection step:

x_{n+1} = P_C (x_n - alpha * G x_n)

Here G denotes the gradient and P_C is the metric projection onto a nonempty closed convex set C. The update rule is short. The harder part is keeping track of exactly which assumptions are needed for each estimate that follows.

For example, the variational inequality used in the convergence proof depends on the properties of the projection and the set. Smoothness can also be written in different ways: a quadratic upper bound is convenient for descent estimates, while other results use a Lipschitz-gradient assumption. I proved the relevant connections separately so they could be used when needed.

How I organised the proofs

I arranged the Isabelle theories roughly in order of their mathematical dependencies, rather than putting everything into one proof of the final result.

The first part covers gradients, convex differentiability, and smoothness: first-order inequalities for convex differentiable functions, quadratic upper bounds, and connections between smoothness assumptions. I then separated out the descent, finite-sum, averaging, and telescoping lemmas that do not depend on a particular algorithm. There is no reason to redo the same summation argument for every first-order method.

Next comes unconstrained gradient descent. The one-step estimates lead to O(1/N) function-value bounds in the smooth convex case, along with results involving gradient residuals.

PGD needs an extra layer of projection geometry. I first established the relevant properties of metric projection and its variational inequality, then used them to prove feasibility preservation, one-step estimates, and finite-horizon convergence bounds.

The projected-gradient mapping is treated separately. A bound on objective values is not, by itself, the same thing as a first-order stationarity certificate. The mapping lets us formulate residual estimates, relate them to optimality conditions, and state epsilon-stationarity complexity results. Finally, adding strong convexity gives the linear convergence results for distance and function value.

I particularly liked working on the residual part. It answers a more specific question than whether the objective decreases: can an iterate give us a quantity that measures how close we are to first-order optimality? What that quantity tells us still depends on the assumptions of the theorem.

Why I added Main_Results.thy

I did not want someone using one PGD convergence theorem to have to understand the whole repository first. So I collected the main results in Main_Results.thy as a common import point.

It provides theorem aliases for gradient descent and PGD convergence bounds, projected-gradient mapping and residual results, and linear convergence under strong convexity. The proofs stay in their respective theory files, while the main results are easier to locate and import.

This is more than a naming change. If I reorganise intermediate lemmas or split theory files later, keeping the public aliases stable should reduce the changes needed in projects that depend on them.

How it connects to the submodular greedy project

In the submodular greedy project, I separated the concrete greedy construction from the abstract step condition, so that lazy greedy could reuse the classical approximation argument. I followed a similar idea here, but the pieces are different: convexity, smoothness, projection, descent estimates, and convergence rates.

The question I wanted to settle was which steps apply to a general descent sequence, which require projection, and which need strong convexity. Stating those dependencies explicitly makes the development easier to check and the intermediate results easier to reuse.

Before publication

The AFP entry also includes quadratic and projected-quadratic examples. In the final stage before submission, I focused on theorem names, Main_Results.thy, theory dependencies, builds, and the AFP document and references. I did not add more algorithms at that point.

After working on this project, I pay more attention to the assumptions behind each convergence claim. When I formalise another first-order method, I would rather identify the lemmas that can stand on their own than copy a handwritten proof line by line into Isabelle.
